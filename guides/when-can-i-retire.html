When Can I Retire? A Practical Guide to Retirement Age

AgeCalculator guides

The question of when you can retire is really two questions: when are you allowed to, and when can you afford to? The first is set by law and by your pension scheme. The second is set by your savings, your spending, and your health. This guide explains both, and how to work out your own retirement date.

State pension age

The state pension age is the earliest age at which you can claim the state pension, and it varies by country and by birth date. In the United Kingdom it is currently 66 and rising to 67 and then 68. In the United States, full Social Security benefits start between 66 and 67 depending on your birth year, with reduced benefits available from 62. In many European countries the age is 65 to 67. The trend everywhere is upward, because people are living longer and the cost of pensions is rising.

Your state pension age is not necessarily the age you must retire — you can usually keep working and delay claiming, which increases the amount you receive. In the UK, delaying the state pension by a year increases it by about 5.8 percent. In the United States, delaying Social Security until age 70 increases the benefit by about 8 percent per year after full retirement age. If you are healthy and enjoy your work, delaying can be the best financial decision you ever make.

Early retirement: the trade-offs

Retiring early sounds wonderful, and for some people it is. But it has a double cost: you stop earning sooner, and your savings have to last longer. Retiring at 55 instead of 65 means ten fewer years of contributions and ten more years of withdrawals, which can easily double the amount you need to have saved. The arithmetic is unforgiving: every year you retire early costs roughly two years of savings.

Early retirement works best for people who have saved aggressively, who own their home, and who have a plan for how they will spend their time. It works worst for people who retire because they are unhappy at work, without a plan for what comes next — boredom and a shrinking bank balance are a bad combination. If you are considering early retirement, try a trial: take a long holiday, live on your expected retirement income for six months, and see how it feels before you hand in your notice.

Working out your number

To find your retirement date, start with your spending. Work out how much you spend in a year, subtract the costs that will disappear when you stop working, and add the costs that will appear — more travel, more leisure, possibly more healthcare. That is your annual retirement income need. Next, add up your expected income: the state pension, any employer pension, and the income from your savings. If the income covers the need, you can retire. If it does not, you need to save more, spend less, or work longer.

A retirement calculator can do this arithmetic for you: enter your birth date, your chosen retirement age, and your savings, and it will show you the date and the gap. But remember that a calculator is only as good as its assumptions. Inflation, investment returns, tax and life expectancy are all uncertain, and small changes in the assumptions produce large changes in the answer. Use the calculator as a guide, not a guarantee, and review your plan every year.

Retiring in stages

The most popular form of retirement today is not a sudden stop but a gradual slow-down. Many people reduce their hours in their sixties, move to part-time or consulting work, or change to a less demanding job. This phased retirement has real advantages: it keeps some income coming in, it keeps you socially connected, and it gives you time to adjust to a life without work. It also lets your pension savings grow for a few more years while you draw less from them.

Some people never fully retire, and that is fine too. Work provides structure, purpose and social contact, and many people are happier with some work than with none. The goal of retirement planning is not to stop working as soon as possible — it is to reach the point where work is a choice rather than a necessity. When you can afford to stop, you are free to continue on your own terms, and that freedom is the real reward of saving.

Frequently asked questions

What is the state pension age?
It varies by country — currently 66 in the UK, rising to 67 and 68; 66–67 in the US for full benefits.

Can I retire at 55?
Yes, if you have saved enough — but retiring ten years early roughly doubles the savings you need.

Should I delay claiming my pension?
Often yes — delaying usually increases the amount you receive each year.
