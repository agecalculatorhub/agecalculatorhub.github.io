Age and Health: What Changes in Your Body by Decade

AgeCalculator guides

Ageing is not a single event but a slow, uneven process that affects every part of the body at a different rate. Your muscles peak in your twenties, your bones in your thirties, your eyesight starts to change in your forties, and your hearing in your fifties. Understanding what changes, and when, helps you take the right action at the right time. This guide walks through the decades and the health steps that matter most in each.

Your 20s: the peak years

Your twenties are the peak of physical performance. Muscle strength, reaction time, lung capacity and bone density are all at or near their best. Your body recovers quickly from exercise, injury and late nights, and your metabolism is forgiving. It is easy to feel invincible, and in many ways you are — but the habits you form now shape your health for decades. Smoking, heavy drinking, poor sleep and a bad diet do damage that is invisible at 25 and obvious at 55.

The most important health steps in your twenties are to build the habits that will carry you through life: regular exercise, a balanced diet, enough sleep, and protection from the sun. Build bone density now with weight-bearing exercise, because bone mass peaks around age 30 and declines after that. Get into the habit of dental check-ups, eye tests and a basic health check, so that problems are caught early. And look after your mental health — anxiety and depression often first appear in the twenties, and early help makes a real difference.

Your 30s and 40s: the turning point

In your thirties, your metabolism slows and your muscle mass begins to decline by about 1 percent a year unless you exercise to maintain it. Your eyesight may start to change, your skin loses some elasticity, and your risk of high blood pressure, high cholesterol and type 2 diabetes begins to rise. These changes are gradual and mostly invisible, which is why regular health checks matter: a blood pressure reading, a cholesterol test and a blood sugar test can catch problems years before they cause symptoms.

The forties are when most people first notice ageing. Reading small print becomes harder, recovery from exercise takes longer, and weight is easier to gain and harder to lose. This is the decade to take strength training seriously, because muscle is the best defence against frailty in old age. It is also the decade when screening becomes important: blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, cervical screening, and — from age 45 or 50 depending on the country — bowel cancer screening. Catching these early saves lives.

Your 50s and 60s: prevention pays off

In your fifties and sixties, the focus shifts to prevention and early detection. Heart disease, stroke, cancer and dementia become the main risks, and the best defences are the same habits that mattered in your twenties: not smoking, regular exercise, a healthy weight, a good diet, moderate alcohol and enough sleep. Exercise remains the most powerful medicine available — regular physical activity reduces the risk of heart disease by about a third, of type 2 diabetes by about a third, and of depression by about a quarter.

This is also the decade when joints, hearing and balance need attention. Hearing loss affects about a third of people over 65, and untreated hearing loss is linked to social isolation and cognitive decline, so get your hearing tested and use hearing aids if you need them. Balance exercises reduce the risk of falls, which are the leading cause of injury in older adults. And keep your mind active: learning, socialising and staying curious are all associated with a lower risk of dementia.

Your 70s and beyond: staying strong

In your seventies and beyond, the goal is to stay independent, mobile and connected. Muscle loss accelerates, so strength training and protein intake become more important, not less. Bones become more fragile, so preventing falls matters: keep your home well lit, remove trip hazards, and have your eyesight and medication reviewed regularly. Social connection is as important as physical health — loneliness is a major risk factor for poor health in old age, and regular contact with friends and family is protective.

The good news is that healthy ageing is not a matter of luck alone. Studies of the longest-lived people in the world — in Okinawa, Sardinia, Ikaria and elsewhere — find the same pattern: a plant-rich diet, daily physical activity, strong social ties, a sense of purpose, and a moderate approach to food and alcohol. None of these requires wealth or special equipment. Ageing is inevitable, but how you age is, to a surprising extent, a choice.

Frequently asked questions

When does muscle mass start to decline?
Around age 30, by about 1 percent a year unless you exercise to maintain it.

What is the most powerful anti-ageing medicine?
Regular exercise — it cuts the risk of heart disease, diabetes and depression.

When should health screening start?
Blood pressure and cholesterol checks in your 30s; cancer screening from your 40s or 50s.
